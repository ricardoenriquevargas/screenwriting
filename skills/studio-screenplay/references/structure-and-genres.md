# Structure and Genre Guide

Read this when building a beat sheet or outline, or diagnosing structural problems.

## Feature structure (≈110 pages)
| Beat | Approx. page | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Opening image | 1 | Tone and the protagonist's world before change |
| Setup | 1–10 | Want vs. need, flaw, stakes, the world's rules |
| Inciting incident | 10–15 | The event that disrupts everything |
| Debate | 15–25 | Resistance; the cost of saying yes |
| Break into Act Two | 25–30 | Active choice into a new situation |
| B-story | ~30 | Relationship that carries the theme |
| Fun and games / promise of the premise | 30–55 | The scenes the trailer is made of |
| Midpoint | ~55 | False victory or false defeat; stakes rise; clock may start |
| Bad guys close in | 55–75 | External pressure plus internal cracks |
| All is lost | ~75 | Lowest point; something or someone dies (literally or symbolically) |
| Dark night of the soul | 75–85 | The protagonist faces the flaw |
| Break into Act Three | ~85 | New plan born from the lesson |
| Finale | 85–108 | Plan executed, twist, protagonist changed |
| Final image | 110 | Mirror of the opening image |

Use this as a diagnostic, not a formula. Name the beats in the user's language.

Alternatives worth knowing: sequence approach (8 sequences of ~12–15 pages, each a mini-movie with its own goal), Hero's Journey for myth-driven stories, Dan Harmon's story circle for character-driven TV.

## Genre notes
- **Drama**: the internal arc carries the story. Every scene needs subtext and a clear emotional shift. Avoid "talking heads"; give characters tasks while they talk.
- **Sports drama**: the big match isn't the climax alone; the real climax is the internal choice made during it. Training montages must show change, not repetition. The opponent should mirror the hero's flaw.
- **Thriller**: information control. Track what the audience knows vs. the protagonist. Ticking clocks, reversals every 10–15 pages, a villain with a plan that works without the hero.
- **Horror**: rules of the threat established early; scares through anticipation; the monster reflects a human fear or trauma; fewer pages (85–100).
- **Comedy**: a strong comic premise with a built-in conflict; characters who are serious inside their absurd situation; set pieces that escalate; still needs an emotional arc.
- **Romance / rom-com**: the obstacle must be internal as well as external; the "meet-cute" sets the dynamic; the breakup at the all-is-lost beat must be earned.
- **Crime / heist**: assembling the team is fun-and-games; the plan explained is the plan that fails; loyalty is the theme.
- **Biopic / true story**: pick one defining chapter, not a life; invent composite scenes honestly; secure rights when portraying living people.
- **Family / animation**: clear want, strong visual world, humor for two audiences, theme stated simply.

## Diagnosing structure problems
- Slow first act → inciting incident too late, or setup without conflict.
- Saggy middle → no midpoint shift, no escalating stakes, B-story missing.
- Flat ending → climax solved by luck or another character; protagonist didn't change.
- Passive protagonist → things happen *to* them; give them choices with costs.
