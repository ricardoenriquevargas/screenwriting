# TV and Series Formats

Read this when the user is writing a pilot, a series, a limited series or a bible.

## Common formats
| Format | Pages | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| One-hour drama (network) | 50–65 | Teaser + 4–5 acts with act-outs (cliffhangers before breaks) |
| One-hour drama (streaming) | 45–65 | Often no formal act breaks, but still structured in 4–5 movements |
| Half-hour single-cam comedy | 25–35 | Cold open + 2–3 acts + tag |
| Multi-cam sitcom | 45–55 | Double-spaced dialogue, scene letters, all-caps action |
| Limited series episode | 45–65 | Each episode a chapter with its own shape plus the series arc |
| Telenovela / serial episode | 25–40 | Multiple plotlines, strong hooks at every break |
| Short-form / vertical series | 2–10 per episode | Hook in the first 10 seconds, cliffhanger every episode |

## Pilot essentials
- Show the engine: why this show can produce 50+ episodes (or, for limited series, why this story needs 6–8 hours).
- Introduce the core cast through action, not exposition dumps.
- Establish the world's rules and the series' central question.
- End the pilot with a hook that makes episode 2 inevitable.
- Premise pilot (origin) vs. non-premise pilot (a typical episode): help the user choose.

## A and B stories
Most episodes run an A-story (main plot), a B-story (secondary, often thematic mirror) and sometimes a C-runner (light, recurring). Track them in the outline as separate columns.

## Series bible (8–20 pages, or a visual deck)
1. Title, logline, format (e.g. "8 × 50' limited drama").
2. Tone and comparables ("*X* meets *Y*").
3. World and setting — why here, why now.
4. Main characters: want, need, flaw, secret, arc across the season.
5. Season one arc, broken into episodes (one paragraph each).
6. Future seasons (a few lines each), or why it ends.
7. Why this creator.

## Writers' room vocabulary
Break the story, beat out the episode, act-out, button, runner, tag, cold open, previously-on, table read, punch-up. Explain terms when the user is new.
